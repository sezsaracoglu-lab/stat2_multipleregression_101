The eleven screens

The short answer to your question

Multiple regression is one outcome predicted from several things at once, in a single equation. Each coefficient is the effect of its own predictor with the others held constant.

The F test asks one question about the whole model: does this set of predictors explain anything at all? A small P-value means at least one slope is not zero — never that all of them matter.

Each t test asks about one predictor: does this one add anything beyond the others? One per row of the coefficients table.

They ask different questions, so they are allowed to disagree — and screens 8 and 9 are about exactly when and why.

📘 What it actually is

One outcome, several things you think affect it, all in one equation.

With one predictor you fit a line. You know this already: ŷ = b₀ + b₁x, and you slide along it.

With two predictors you fit a tilted sheet: ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂. Instead of sliding along a line you move across a surface — forwards for more x₁, sideways for more x₂.

With five predictors the picture is impossible to draw, but nothing else changes. Everything you learned about the line carries over — only the one-predictor shortcuts (F = t², Beta = r) stop working. You just add more b’s.

The equation, and what each bit is

  • ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂ + … + bₚxₚ
  • ŷ — the predicted outcome. Never an observed value; the hat is the whole difference.
  • b₀ — the intercept. Predicted y when every x is zero.
  • b₁, b₂, … — one slope per predictor. Each one is the effect of its own x with the others held still.
  • p — how many predictors. This letter runs the whole theme: it sets the degrees of freedom, the parameter count and the F test.

Say it to a friend

  • “Simple regression draws a line through a cloud of dots. Multiple regression does the same thing with more than one predictor — so instead of a line you get a tilted surface, and instead of one slope you get one slope per predictor.”

The hook

  • One predictor is a line. Two is a sheet. Five is the same arithmetic with more letters.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Thinking multiple regression is a different technique. It is not. It is the same least-squares idea — minimise the squared vertical gaps — with more than one x. Every habit from Theme 4 still applies, including looking at the residual plot before you believe anything.

🌳 Why you would ever want two

Because some questions genuinely cannot be answered with one number.

A forester wants to know how much usable wood is in a tree. She can measure height and she can measure width.

Height alone will not do it: two trees of the same height can hold completely different amounts of wood, depending on how thick they are. Width alone will not do it either, for exactly the same reason.

Put both in one equation and the problem disappears. That is the entire motivation for multiple regression — not statistical cleverness, just the fact that one measurement often is not enough.

Three things that happen when you add a predictor

  • 1. R² never falls — it goes up or stays put, even if the new predictor is useless. So a rise proves nothing on its own.
  • 2. You lose a degree of freedom — the error df drops by one, so the predictor has to earn its keep: unless it explains enough to pull MSE down, you have paid in precision for nothing.
  • 3. Every other coefficient can change — because each one is now answering a slightly different question.
  • Those three together are why you do not just throw in every variable you measured.

Say it to a friend

  • “Some outcomes have more than one cause, and the causes are not interchangeable. A tall thin tree and a short fat tree have the same amount of wood — you need both measurements to tell them apart, so you put both in the equation.”

The hook

  • If one number cannot distinguish two cases you care about, you need a second predictor.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Adding predictors because they are available rather than because they answer something. Each one costs a degree of freedom and makes every coefficient harder to interpret. A model with four well-chosen predictors beats one with twelve.

🎚️ “Holding the others constant”

The one phrase that makes a multiple regression coefficient mean anything.

Say you predict exam score from hours of study and hours of sleep. The coefficient b₁ on study is not “how much score rises per hour of study”.

It is: how much score rises per hour of study among people who sleep the same amount. The picture shows it — three fixed amounts of sleep, three parallel lines, and b₁ is the slope of each one.

That is why these are called partial slopes. Each one has the other predictors frozen behind it. Drop the phrase and you have described a different model — the simple regression on study alone — which would give a different number.

The sentence that always works

  • “One unit more of this x goes with b units more predicted y, on average, with the other predictors held constant.”
  • Four parts, and every one is load-bearing:
  • one unit more — a comparison, not a change you made happen.
  • predicted y — the surface, not any one person.
  • on average — individuals scatter around it.
  • others held constant — the part that makes it partial.

Say it to a friend

  • “Each coefficient compares two people who are identical on everything else in the model and differ by one unit on that one thing. It is a like-for-like comparison, built into the arithmetic.”

The hook

  • Same everything else, one unit more of this.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Saying “causes”. Holding other variables constant is not the same as a controlled experiment — it only holds constant the variables you actually measured. Anything you did not measure is still free to vary, and still free to be the real explanation.

Check before you move on

  • Cover the words “holding the others constant” with your thumb and read your sentence again. If it still sounds true, you have said something about a different model.

🧰 Every job it does

Five different questions, one machine. The question is what changes.

People talk about multiple regression as if it were one thing. It is really five jobs, and knowing which one you are doing tells you which number to look at.

Predict and combine only need the model to fit well — R² and s. Weigh up needs the standardised Betas. Control for and compare models are where the thinking is, and where the exam questions live.

In psychology the big one is control for: does my variable still predict the outcome once age, sex and the obvious confounders are in the model? That is a multiple regression, and it is most of what you will read in a journal.

The five jobs, and the number each one needs

  • 1. Predict — substitute into the equation. Watch R² and s; check the x values are inside the data.
  • 2. Weigh up which matters most — the standardised Beta column. Raw coefficients in different units cannot be compared. Betas rank predictors inside this model, on this sample — not across models or samples, and not reliably at all when the predictors overlap.
  • 3. Control for something — the t and P of your predictor, with the controls already in the model.
  • 4. Combine weak predictors — the overall F, which can be significant when no single t is.
  • 5. Compare two models — adjusted R², or the R²-change test if one model contains the other.

Predicting: the two intervals are not the same

  • Both are centred on the same ŷ. The difference is what you are predicting.
  • Confidence interval for a mean response — the average y of everyone with these x values. Only the uncertainty in the surface.
  • Prediction interval for an individual — one new person with these x values. The uncertainty in the surface plus that person’s own scatter around it.
  • So the prediction interval is always the wider of the two, at the same x values and the same confidence level. “An individual” → wider.

Say it to a friend

  • “It predicts, it ranks predictors, it lets you ask whether something still matters after you have allowed for the obvious alternatives, it lets weak predictors team up, and it lets you ask whether a bigger model was worth it. Same machine, five questions.”

The hook

  • Decide which of the five jobs you are doing, and the output tells you which number to read.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Reading the whole printout and then not knowing what to say. Pick the job first. If you are controlling for age, the only number that answers your question is your own predictor’s P — not R², not F.

🔢 The fundamentals, on one screen

Six numbers. If you can produce these, you can answer almost any question on this theme.

Everything in this theme comes out of one bar cut in two. All the variation in y is SST. The part the model explains is SSM. What is left over is SSE.

From those two pieces plus n and p you can build the entire ANOVA table, R², s, every degree of freedom, and the F statistic. Nothing else is needed.

If an exam gives you an output table with a hole in it, this is the picture to have in your head: fill the DF column first, get the missing SS by subtraction, then divide down the rows.

The six numbers, in the order you build them

  • 1. Degrees of freedom. Model = p. Error = n − p − 1. Total = n − 1. The two rows must add to the total.
  • 2. Sums of squares. SST = SSM + SSE. Any missing one comes by subtraction.
  • 3. Mean squares. MS = SS ⁄ DF, inside one row. Never across rows.
  • 4. F = MS model ⁄ MS error. Mean squares, never sums of squares.
  • 5. R² = SSM ⁄ SST. The explained share of the variation in y.
  • 6. s = √MS error. The typical distance of a case from the surface, in the units of y.

Two counts people muddle

  • Predictors = p. Five x variables → p = 5. This is the model df and the first number of the F test.
  • Parameters = p + 1. Five x variables → six b’s, because the intercept counts. This is what comes off n.
  • So with n = 18 and five predictors: model df = 5, error df = 18 − 5 − 1 = 12, total df = 17. And 5 + 12 = 17 ✓

Say it to a friend

  • “All the variation in the outcome is one bar. The model explains part of it and misses the rest. R-squared is the share it explains; s is how far a typical case sits from the surface; and the ANOVA table is just that bar written as numbers.”

The hook

  • df first, then subtract, then divide down the rows.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Dividing SSM by SSE to get F. It gives a plausible-looking number and it is wrong — both have to be turned into mean squares first, because one was built from p degrees of freedom and the other from n − p − 1.

Check before you move on

  • Model df + error df = total df = n − 1. If that does not hold, stop — something is wrong before you compute a single mean square.

🅕 The F test: is there anything here at all?

One question, about the whole model, with a yes-or-no answer.

The F test asks exactly one thing: do these predictors, taken together, explain anything? Not which ones. Not how much. Just whether the set does any work.

Its null hypothesis is that every slope is zero — the model is useless. A small P-value rejects that, so you conclude the opposite: at least one slope is not zero.

That is the whole content. “At least one” is deliberately weak, and getting it right is worth marks: F can never tell you that all the predictors matter.

The procedure

  • H₀: β₁ = β₂ = … = βₚ = 0 — none of them helps.
  • H₁: at least one β is not zero.
  • F = MS model ⁄ MS error, on (p, n − p − 1) degrees of freedom — a pair, model first.
  • Compare with the critical value, or just read the P. Small P → reject H₀.
  • The test is one-sided: F cannot be negative, a useless model gives F near 1, and a useful one gives F large. All of α sits in the upper tail.

Say it to a friend

  • “The F test is the bouncer at the door. It tells you whether the group gets in at all. It does not tell you anything about any individual in the group.”

The hook

  • F significant → AT LEAST ONE slope is non-zero. Never “all of them”.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Two of them, and both are offered as options in exams:
  • • reading a significant F as “all the slopes are non-zero”;
  • • giving F a single degree of freedom. It always takes two, in the order (predictors, error). Swap them and you read the wrong cell of the table entirely.

📐 The t tests: does THIS one earn its place?

One question per predictor, each one asked with the others already in the model.

Every row of the coefficients table is its own t test. It asks: does this predictor add anything beyond the others?

The arithmetic is one division: t = Coef ⁄ SE Coef, on n − p − 1 degrees of freedom — the same df for every row, intercept included. For any sample worth having, |t| above about 2 is significant at 5%.

The words “beyond the others” are not decoration. A predictor can be significant on its own and not in company, or the reverse. Each t belongs to this model and says nothing about any other.

The procedure

  • H₀: βⱼ = 0 — this predictor adds nothing, given the rest.
  • t = Coef ⁄ SE Coef, within the predictor’s own row.
  • df = n − p − 1, which is the Residual Error row of the ANOVA table — you never compute it twice.
  • Two-sided, so a 95% interval uses the 0.025 column of the t table.
  • Confidence interval: b ± t* · SE(b). If it excludes zero, P < α. The multiplier is t* from the t table at df = n − p − 1 — never a z from the Normal table.

Say it to a friend

  • “Each t asks whether that one predictor is still pulling its weight once the others are already in. It is a question about this model, not about the predictor in general.”

The hook

  • Run your finger down the T column and ring any row whose |t| is under 2.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Judging a predictor by the size of its coefficient. A coefficient’s size depends entirely on the units of its predictor — change minutes to hours and it multiplies by sixty without anything about the study changing. Judge each coefficient against its own standard error, which is what t and P are.

Check before you move on

  • Pick one row and divide Coef by SE Coef. If it matches the printed t, you are reading the right columns. If it is wildly out, you have the wrong column; if it is out in the last digit, that is just rounding.

⚖️ F or t? The difference, settled

Different questions, different scopes, different hypotheses. They can disagree.

This is the question you asked, and here is the short answer: F is about the set, each t is about one predictor with the others still in.

Because they ask different things, all four combinations happen. The one worth knowing is F says yes while no t says yes: the predictors overlap, so removing any single one changes little, yet removing all of them loses the explanation.

So a disagreement is information, not an arithmetic error. The honest report in that case is: the model explains the outcome, and these data cannot separate the individual contributions.

Side by side

  • Scope. F: the whole model. t: one predictor.
  • Null. F: every slope is zero. t: this slope is zero.
  • Statistic. F = MS model ⁄ MS error. t = Coef ⁄ SE Coef.
  • Degrees of freedom. F: a pair, (p, n − p − 1). t: a single number, n − p − 1.
  • Tails. F: one-sided, all α in the upper tail. t: two-sided.
  • How many. F: exactly one per model. t: one per coefficient.
  • Answers. F: “is there anything here?” t: “does this one add anything?”

When there is only ONE predictor

  • They become the same test. “Is this slope zero?” and “does the model explain anything?” are then the same question, and
  • F = t², with identical P-values.
  • Two more shortcuts hold only then: Beta = r and R² = Beta².
  • All three break the moment you add a second predictor — that is a favourite exam question.

Say it to a friend

  • “F asks about the team; each t asks about one player, with the rest still on the field. The team can be winning while no individual looks essential — which is exactly what happens when the players do similar jobs.”

The hook

  • F opens the door; the t tests tell you who came in.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Assuming F and the t tests must agree, and hunting for an arithmetic error when they do not. They are answering different questions. Overlapping predictors routinely give a crushing F and not one significant t.

🪑 Why they can disagree

Four legs holding one table. Any one is removable; all four are not.

If predictors carry much the same information, each one is nearly redundant given the others. Take any single leg away and the table still stands — so no individual t looks essential.

Take all four away and the table falls. That is what F measures: the set, all at once. The name for predictors that overlap like this is multicollinearity.

This also explains a thing that worries people: adding a predictor can make another one’s coefficient shrink, or flip sign, or stop being significant. Nothing has gone wrong — the question that coefficient answers has changed.

What to report when they disagree

  • • Say the model as a whole explains the outcome (that is the F).
  • • Say the data cannot separate the individual contributions (that is the t’s).
  • • Do not report every predictor as significant, and do not report nothing. Both halves of the sentence are true and both are needed.

Say it to a friend

  • “If two predictors measure almost the same thing, each one looks dispensable because the other covers for it. Together they are doing real work. That is why the overall test and the individual tests can point different ways.”

The hook

  • A coefficient means nothing until you say what else is in the model.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Dropping a predictor because adding another one made it non-significant, and calling that a cleaner model. It is a different model answering a different question. Decide which question you want first.

🥧 R² and adjusted R²

One describes a model. The other compares models of different sizes.

R² is the share of the variation in y that the model accounts for. It is a share of the variation, and nothing else — not whether the shape is right, not a distance in the units of y (that is s), and not an accuracy per person.

Its weakness: it cannot fall when you add a predictor, however useless. So a rise tells you nothing, and you cannot use it to choose between a small model and a big one.

Adjusted R² charges a fee for every predictor. It can fall, and when it does, the extra predictors did not pay for themselves. That is the one and only job it has — and it is the one R² cannot do.

Which to quote, when

  • Describing one model: quote R². “64% of the variation in y is accounted for by the regression on these predictors.”
  • Comparing models with different numbers of predictors: quote adjusted R². It is the only one of the two that can answer that.
  • Comparing two models where one contains the other: the R²-change test, which asks whether the extra block bought more than it cost. It is an F test, not a t test: F = (extra SSM ⁄ q) ⁄ MSE of the big model, on df = q and n − p − 1, where q is how many predictors you added and p is the big model’s total.
  • The diagnostic is adjusted R² falling. The gap between R² and adjusted R² grows with p whatever the predictors are worth, so the gap on its own tells you nothing.

Say it to a friend

  • “R-squared always goes up when you add a variable, so it cannot tell you whether the variable was worth adding. Adjusted R-squared takes a fee per variable, so if it goes down, the variable did not pay for itself.”

The hook

  • R² describes one model; adjusted R² compares two of different sizes.

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Three, all common in exam options:
  • • calling R² an accuracy — it is a share of variation across the sample, not a per-case hit rate;
  • • calling it a share of people — every case has a residual;
  • • treating a high R² as proof the model is right. A high R² sits happily on a curve. Only the residual plot can tell you the shape is wrong.

🔍 Before you believe any of it

Four conditions. Check them first, read the P-values second.

Every P-value and interval on the printout is computed as if four things were true. If they are not, the numbers are still printed and they still mean nothing.

Two of the four you check with a residual plot — residuals against the fitted values, since with several predictors there is no single x to use. Featureless scatter is good. An arch or a U means the shape is wrong. A fan means the spread is not constant. Normality is a third plot: a histogram or Normal quantile plot of the residuals.

Independence the residual plot cannot show you at all. It is a question about how the data were collected, and it is the one that inflates your confidence rather than merely distorting it.

The four conditions

  • 1. Linear — the relationship is a straight surface. Residual plot: no arch, no U.
  • 2. Normal — y is normally distributed at each combination of the x’s. Not y overall, and nothing at all is required of the x’s. Checked on a histogram or Normal quantile plot of the residuals, not on the residual-versus-fitted plot.
  • 3. Equal spread — the same scatter at every point on the surface. Residual plot: no fan.
  • 4. Independent — one row per independent case. Checked from the study design, never from a plot.

Say it to a friend

  • “The model always gives you numbers. The conditions decide whether the numbers mean anything. Look at the residual plot before you look at a single P-value.”

The hook

  • Plot first, P-value second. A shape in the residuals overrules any R².

The mistake almost everyone makes

  • Thinking independence is about the variables. It is about the rows. Twelve children measured on five days gives you sixty rows and twelve independent cases — analysing it as n = 60 makes every standard error too small, every t too big, and every P-value too small. The residual plot will look perfect throughout.

The order to work in

  • 1. Look at the residual plot. 2. Ask how the data were collected. 3. Read R² and s. 4. Read F. 5. Read the t column. Reversing 1 and 5 is how people end up reporting a confident number from a model that does not apply.
